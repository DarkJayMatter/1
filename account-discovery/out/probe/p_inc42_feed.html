Indian retail investors rushed to buy and trade Anthropic shares following its Nasdaq debut yesterday, with the Claude maker drawing substantial activity across overseas investing platforms Mudrex, INDmoney, and Vested.

Anthropic accounted for around 40% of INDmoney’s international investment activity on its markets debut day, while its average order value was twice the platform’s average, according to the company.

On Mudrex, around 35% of daily active users traded Anthropic shares. Trading activity was 20-30% higher than on a typical day, with the platform describing the response as comparable to that recorded during the SpaceX IPO.

The average ticket size stood at ₹2,000-₹3,000, while several investors also set up systematic investment plans (SIPs) to build longer-term exposure, Mudrex said.

“The response to Anthropic’s listing reflects the growing interest among Indian investors in accessing global assets, particularly companies at the forefront of emerging technologies such as AI,” said Prateek Gupta, head of business at Mudrex.

Gupta added that investors are increasingly looking beyond domestic markets to diversify their portfolios and build long-term exposure to global assets.

On Vested, Anthropic accounted for approximately 11% of total traded value and 9% of all trades during the session. It ranked second by traded value, behind AMD and ahead of NVIDIA, according to the platform.

Activity was concentrated in the opening hours, with 84% of Anthropic trades on Vested placed within the first two hours. The average buy order stood at around $1,100.

Buying Into The AI Boom

For much of the AI boom, direct investments in companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI have been largely limited to VC firms, institutional investors, and wealthy individuals. Anthropic’s listing opens a route for retail investors to own shares in a company developing frontier AI.

Overseas investing platforms facilitate access to US-listed companies for Indian investors, with eligible individuals able to invest through routes such as the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), subject to applicable regulatory and tax requirements.

Indian retail investors using platforms such as Zerodha, INDmoney, and Vested can buy US stocks after listing but cannot subscribe directly to their IPOs through these platforms. Their entry price therefore depends on the market price when they buy, rather than the IPO offer price.

Anthropic’s debut illustrated this price gap. Its shares opened at $111.50, a 34.3% premium to the $83 IPO price, before closing at $97.17 – up 17.1% from the issue price but down 12.9% from the opening price.

The IPO was worth approximately $81.3 Bn, including shares sold by existing shareholders, and valued Anthropic at around $1.5 Tn at the offer price. The company also lined up $26 Bn in concurrent private placements involving technology partners, including Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA.

The debut came amid concerns about the cost of developing advanced AI systems and the returns companies can generate from infrastructure spending. Anthropic shares reportedly fell more than 3% in after-hours trading yesterday following reports that Apple was developing an autonomous AI agent, adding to competition from OpenAI and Meta.

However, Anthropic shares fell around 10% in pre-market trading today amid a broader sell-off in AI-linked stocks, reversing part of yesterday’s debut gains.

India’s AI Ambitions

Beyond retail trading, the listing has reignited debate about India’s ability to build globally competitive AI companies.

Anthropic’s $1.5 Tn valuation at the IPO price is equivalent to nearly 37% of India’s projected nominal GDP of $4.06 Tn for FY27, according to the IMF’s October estimates. The comparison illustrates scale, although a company’s valuation and an economy’s annual output measure different things.

Veteran banker and Kotak Mahindra Bank founder Uday Kotak recently compared Anthropic’s valuation with the combined market capitalisation of India’s top 10 companies, questioning whether Indian businesses were undervalued or whether the country needed to catch up with the emerging technology economy.

The debate comes amid rising investment in India’s AI ecosystem. Indian AI startups raised $676 Mn across 57 deals in H1 2026, more than 4X the funding recorded in the year-ago period. The sector attracted another $438 Mn across 35 deals in Q3, taking funding for the first nine months of the year to over $1.1 Bn.

Building companies at Anthropic’s scale, however, will require sustained investment in research, computing infrastructure, technical talent, and product development.

In a major relief for ride-hailing platforms such as Rapido and Uber, the Supreme Court (SC) has dismissed the Karnataka government’s appeal against a Karnataka High Court (HC) ruling permitting bike taxi operations in the state.

A bench comprising Justices Satish Chandra Sharma and N Kotiswar Singh upheld the HC’s January 23 judgment, which lifted the suspension on bike taxi services in Karnataka.

During the hearing, Justice Sharma highlighted the affordability of bike taxis, citing the experience of young women working at the Supreme Court who use two-wheelers to commute because of the lower fares.

The ruling is expected to provide regulatory clarity for bike taxi operators in Karnataka, where services have faced legal uncertainty over the classification and registration of motorcycles used for commercial passenger transport.

It must be noted that the bike taxi framework may vary from state across India. Bike taxi regulations vary across states because transport policy and permit conditions are implemented by individual state governments under the broader framework of the Motor Vehicles Act. While the central law has a broader framework, the onus of framing state-specific rules and regulations lies on local authorities.  

This has led to different approaches, with some states allowing the services subject to local regulations, while others have restricted or prohibited them. For instance, states like Maharashtra, Kerala, Delhi, have put in place bike taxi adverse regulations over the past few years. 

In a bid to bring in some uniformity on a nation-wide scale, union minister for road transport and highways Nitin Gadkari urged states to permit bike taxis by adopting the Motor Vehicle Aggregator Guidelines, 2025. The minister cited the potential to expand livelihood opportunities amid crackdowns on bike taxi operations across several states.

“If the whole country accepts this, then 10 Cr jobs will be created. And the food and other delivery apps working in this are different. So, if 10 Cr and 8-10 Cr are there, then 18 Cr jobs will be created here,” the minister said yesterday. 

Reacting to the SC’s decision, Uber said the decision brings “much-needed regulatory clarity” and recognises bike taxis as an affordable and convenient mobility option in congested cities.

For Uber, the ruling comes at a time when it is doubling down on bike taxis across the country. Facing tight competition from category leader, Rapido, Uber recently made a public announcement confirming its bike taxi service, Uber Bike, to 100 cities in India. Uber claimed in August that it is now present in more than 220 Indian cities.

Meanwhile, Rapido’s cofounder Pavan Guntupalli pointed to the larger economic benefits of the SC ruling today. He believes that it creates value for commuters, drivers and cities, adding that the startup would look forward to working with the Karnataka government to develop the category within a framework focused on safety and regulation.

“For Rapido, this is more than a legal outcome. It is an affirmation of something we have consistently believed in: that India needs mobility solutions designed around the way people actually travel,” Guntupalli said.

Bike taxis have been the bread and butter for the Prosus-backed unicorn.  Rapido built its business around bike taxis, using the spare capacity of privately owned motorcycles to offer affordable rides without investing in its own fleet. The model helped it scale to over 400 cities and facilitate more than 5 Mn rides daily, while supporting over 9 Mn livelihoods.

Karnataka’s Bike Taxi Dispute

At the heart of the matter is an erstwhile order passed by a single judge bench of the Karnataka HC, directing bike taxi operators to suspend services until the state government introduced a regulatory policy permitting their operations.

The order was challenged by ride-hailing platforms Ola, Uber and Rapido, motorcycle owners, and the Bike Taxi Welfare Association.

On January 23, a Division Bench of the Karnataka HC, comprising Chief Justice Vibhu Bakhru and Justice C M Joshi, set aside the earlier order and allowed bike taxi operations to resume.

The bench held that motorcycles could be registered as transport vehicles and operated as contract carriages. It also observed that while the state government could frame regulations governing bike taxis, it could not refuse permits solely on the ground that the vehicles were two-wheelers.

The Karnataka government subsequently challenged the HC’s decision before the Supreme Court, seeking to overturn the order permitting bike taxi operations.

With the apex court dismissing the appeal, the HC’s decision remains in force, allowing bike taxi services to operate in Karnataka, subject to applicable regulatory requirements.

For platforms such as Rapido, Uber and Ola, Karnataka is an important market for app-based mobility services. The ruling also comes amid wider efforts by ride-hailing companies to expand two-wheeler-based transport offerings, which typically cater to commuters seeking lower-cost alternatives to autos and cabs.

However, the SC’s decision upholding the HC order does not rule out the Karnataka government’s ability to frame and enforce regulations for the category.

It is rare for a prominent Indian VC firm to go through a churn at the top without people on the ground in Bengaluru and Delhi talking about what might be happening. And so it is with Fireside Ventures. 

“A single partner leaving is pretty much routine. Two top level exits in a matter of weeks with even the resumes of junior staff floating around in rival firms surely means something more,” an investor associated with a consumer tech VC firm told Inc42, referring to the recent sequence of events at Fireside Ventures. 

But there could be more at stake here than just the two exits, even if that is where the focus currently lies.

Earlier this month, when Fireside Ventures announced that cofounder and partner Vinay Singh would step down to explore “the frontiers of agentic commerce”, the move was widely seen as a personal one. Singh was said to be leaving to pursue what he believed was the next big shift in consumption. 

His exit, however, could not be viewed in isolation when, just 10 days later, as Inc42 had exclusively reported, Prayag Mohanty, a principal at the firm who had led several of Fireside’s early-stage bets, also moved out.

Two senior exits in under two weeks would be a talking point at any VC firm. The two departures and the succession question have created a buzz in VC circles about the future of Fireside, one of India’s largest consumer focused VC firms with ₹5,300 Cr in AUM

Fireside does run a deliberately lean investment team, but multiple people Inc42 spoke with said the two exits after closing a ₹2,265 Cr fund less than a year ago, in December 2025, cannot be considered a coincidence and that the “real story” lies at the very top.

Kanwaljit Singh, the founder and managing partner who has been the face of Fireside since he set it up in 2017, has expressed his intent to move away from the firm’s day-to-day operations. He may take on a mentor or chairman role, though the exact contours have not been decided yet, sources said.

“Kanwaljit has told some of the founders in Fireside’s portfolio that Fund IV could be the last fund he is associated with. This is truly a big move for the entire VC firm, and handing over the baton to the top executives has been in the works for a while now,” people aware of these discussions told us.

That has opened up the most consequential question a founder-led fund can face: Who comes next?

According to the people Inc42 spoke with, the answer has not been settled.

The process of finding the next leader has triggered differences within the firm, with at least two senior executives, Kannan Sitaram and Dipanjan Basu, who were elevated to the positions of cofounders, being seen as successors.

Fireside currently lists Kanwaljit Singh, Sitaram, Basu and Adarsh Menon as partners. Besides these, the firm has five principals who, along with the partners, make up the deal-flow bench.

The succession plan under way at the VC firm paints cofounder and partner Vinay Singh’s departure in a different light. If the firm is indeed looking for someone to take over the reins, it would have made sense to pass the torch to Vinay Singh, who cofounded the firm.

Fireside Ventures did not respond to Inc42’s queries by the time of publishing this report.

Two Exits, One Pattern

Viewed individually, the two departures do not appear to be directly linked to the succession process currently under way at Fireside.

Vinay Singh, an IIM Bangalore alumnus who worked at Hindustan Unilever and BankBazaar and founded car servicing platform Stepni before Fireside Ventures, had said publicly that AI was reshaping commerce “faster than most of us expected”. 

In particular, he had a strong view on agentic commerce, and remained a leading voice from Fireside championing the agentic commerce wave in India’s consumer tech story. That conviction is being seen as a trigger for his departure. 

But his move is a little surprising as he could still have pursued his agentic commerce vision without exiting Fireside, especially when the newer bets in consumer tech are increasingly concentrated towards AI now.

Mohanty, meanwhile, is understood to be preparing to launch his own consumer fund, according to people familiar with his plans. This would be a natural next step in his journey as an investor. 

Mohanty has invested in early stage startups since 2013, first as a Principal at IvyCap Ventures and, since January 2020, at Fireside. He drove several of the firm’s seed stage bets over the past year, including a ₹8 Cr round in Gurugram-based men’s personal care brand Unbound, a ₹20 Cr round in Noida-based Oroos Confectionery and another ₹8 Cr seed round in kids wellness startup Wellopia.

But the people Inc42 spoke with say the timing of these exits tells a “bigger story”. 

“Any VC firm where plans of succession are being talked about does experience a churn, especially when the lifecycle of a new fund easily spans 7-10 years. That is the normal course. If Kanwal passes the baton to younger folks, disagreements over who should lead the deployment can be part of the process. And this is where the churn is expected,” an operator-turned-senior partner at a Mumbai-based VC firm said.

 

The churn, in fact, may not end with just the two departures. Some other members of the investment team are also exploring opportunities outside Fireside, two separate sources divulged to us, 

Singh has always maintained that the firm deliberately keeps its investing team small and builds out a “centre of excellence” around it, spanning go-to-market, marketing, corporate development, ESG, finance and HR. 

“In a firm of that size, losing a cofounder and one of five principals in the same month is not a marginal change. It is a fifth of the deal-facing bench, especially when they have been your faces for some key deals,” an investment banker who has worked in one of the Big Four consultancy firms said on condition of anonymity. 

The Kanwaljit Singh Factor

To understand why Kanwaljit Singh’s potential step back is causing ripples, it is important to understand how central he has been to Fireside Ventures. 

The Fireside founder came to the venture capital world from retail and FMCG. He spent 13 years at Hindustan Unilever, from 1987 to 2000, across sales, marketing and product innovation. He then spent three years as general manager (marketing) at Intel for India and the SAARC region, and was a director at The Carlyle Group’s India technology practice from 2001 to 2005.

His first brush with the VC world was in 2005, when he cofounded Helion Venture Partners. There, he spent a decade leading consumer and consumer tech investments for the firm which had an AUM of about $600 Mn. 

In 2017, when most Indian VCs were still chasing internet marketplaces, he set up Fireside Ventures to do something not many else had thought of: back new-age D2C consumer brands at the earliest stage. And the bets paid off spectacularly. 

Fireside Ventures was an early backer of boAt and Mamaearth, at a time when the D2C as a category barely existed in India. Mamaearth’s parent Honasa Consumer went public in 2023 and boAt’s parent Imagine Marketing has filed for a ₹1,500 Cr IPO. 

The VC firm’s portfolio now spans 65+ brands, including The Sleep Company, Pilgrim, Traya, Yoga Bar, The Good Bug, Slurrp Farm and Inito. Fireside says it was the first institutional investor in over 74% of its portfolio companies. Across the platform, Fireside says its portfolio generates more than $1.6 Bn in revenue and is worth over $7 Bn.

Naturally, for those on the other side of the table — whether limited partners (LPs) or founders — it was hard to separate Fireside from Kanwaljit Singh. “Fireside is Kanwal and Kanwal is Fireside. You have to give it to him for spotting such winners and for the fund’s LP and investor relationships. He had an eye for consumer deals when D2C was in its infancy in India,” a senior partner at a rival VC firm acknowledged.

The returns also back this. Fireside’s Fund I ranked first among 11 Category I AIFs of the 2018 vintage in CRISIL’s AIF benchmarks, based on INR IRR and DPI, as of September 2024.

And underpinning all of it is the money Singh has raised. 

According to people familiar with the VC, every one of Fireside’s four funds was raised largely on the strength of Kanwaljit Singh’s personal relationships and track record.

The shift in Fund IV, from a largely domestic LP base to sovereign wealth funds and global institutions, is important to note. “Institutional LPs tend to underwrite people as much as track records, and they scrutinise key-person risk closely,” one senior investor quoted above added.

Fireside has also been unusually disciplined about fund size. 

In July 2026, Kanwaljit Singh reportedly said that Fireside Ventures could have raised “2x” its fourth fund but chose not to, arguing that a bigger fund would break the approach that generates returns. He also described his role as having evolved into “a more classic CEO role”, driving vision, strategy and culture. 

Partners, he said, run sectors and investment stages. “Today, it is all about legacy,” he said, adding that the aim was to “build a lot of bench strength to keep the legacy going”. 

In that light, a transition may have been in the works for a while. The question is whether the rest of Fireside was ready for it, and whether everyone agrees on who should lead.

People Inc42 spoke with described Fireside Ventures as, in effect, “Kanwaljit’s brand”.

He holds most of the  decision-making power and his legacy relationships with LPs and the biggest founders in Fireside’s portfolio. So, whoever takes over from Singh will have to convince these LPs and founders that all that the Fireside playbook is about the system and the experience that comes with investing over the course of a decade, and not just about one person. 

“The biggest answer needs to be given to the LPs and institutional investors when the transition of this sort takes place. Unlike the leadership transition in any startup or corporate where the issues will be solved between board and management, the investors involved here can question the deployment lifecycle of the fund among many other issues,” the Bengaluru-based senior investor quoted above added.

It’s not yet clear whether Singhs’s plans have been formally communicated to Fireside’s LPs as of now, company sources told Inc42.

The Carry Question

When senior partners leave Indian VC firms, the conversation usually turns to carry – the share of a fund’s profits that goes to its managers. 

As Inc42 reported earlier this year and in the past, carry is the main breaking point among partners in large firms, particularly when there is a gap in the vintage of the partners. Those on the founding side usually come with years of experience and decades of relationships, but new partners and new principals tend to carry forward the baton when a new generation of investment opportunities emerge. 

Naturally, fund managers want a larger share of the upside they helped create, and also want the track record attribution that comes with it.

However, at Fireside, it is not yet clear whether carry is a factor. But according to some investors and partners at other firms Inc42 spoke with, it could turn into a crucial issue now that a succession plan appears to be under way. 

In a founder-led fund, the founder often holds a large share of the carry, reflecting the role in raising the money. When that founder steps back, carry for the current and future funds has to be redistributed. That makes the succession question an economic and commercial one as much as one about leadership and relationships.

Kanwaljit Singh has said publicly that compensation at Fireside Ventures is “shared across the team” rather than concentrated on whoever backed the biggest winner. He also once revealed that multiple people work on each portfolio company under a ‘One Fireside’ approach, so that no single person is the sole point of contact. Whether that philosophy survives a change in guard will be a key question.

Notably, the debate around partner payouts is playing out globally too. 

Earlier this month, Sequoia Capital said in a memo that it had made several internal changes that have the effect of “flattening the partnership”.  The firm is now owned and operated by seven general partners under the stewardship of Alfred Lin and Pat Grady, and said it wants “every partner to flourish”. 

Sequoia’s restructuring has nothing to do with Fireside, but one senior corporate governance advisor told us that it “shows the direction in which LPs and younger partners are pushing away from founder-centred economics and towards a more evenly shared model”.

The succession plan has one other important question: whether Singh’s move would invoke the key-person clause in Fireside’s LP agreements.

Notably, most institutional VC funds carry such a clause, also called the ‘key person’ clause. It names individuals whose continued involvement is critical for LPs, and it is part of the fiduciary responsibility of the firm’s partners. If one of the key persons leaves, or substantially reduces their time commitment, a ‘key person event’ clause may be triggered. Typically, the fund’s investment period is then suspended until limited partners agree on a replacement.

One of the investors that Inc42 spoke with for this story said a founder moving to a chairman or mentor role is the kind of change that could test such provisions. LPs would then look at how much of the committed corpus will be deployed, and whether the firm has enough senior executives left to keep investing, especially after two senior exits. 

There is no indication yet that any such clause will be triggered at Fireside Ventures, or that a change has been communicated to its LPs. The terms of Fireside’s LP agreements are not public, but investors in other firms typically have a fair idea of what these agreements contain. 

Fireside’s Fund IV, meanwhile, is early in its deployment cycle. It plans to back 30-35 brands from it, with initial cheques of $1 Mn to $12 Mn, and Kanwaljiti Singh has said he prefers to deploy each fund in roughly three years. 

The firm has stayed active this year, leading ₹30 Cr rounds in maternal care brand Promom and D2C grocery startup Anmasa.

The Spin-Out Season

The churn at Fireside Ventures coincides with a wave of senior partners leaving established VC firms to set up new ones. 

Ashish Agrawal, Ishaan Mittal and Tejeshwi Sharma left Peak XV Partners to set up Mettle Capital, targeting $350-400 Mn. Shailesh Lakhani, Harshjit Sethi and Mayank Porwal, also former Peak XV partners, launched Ambition Capital which is looking to raise $250 Mn for its maiden fund.  

These exits came down to a mix of carry, autonomy and the question of who writes the firm’s next chapter. Fireside’s situation is obviously different, but the core challenges are more or less the same.

According to people Inc42 spoke with, several of the newly floated funds are actively looking to hire experienced principals and partners from established firms. This also attracts some principals and lower level investment team members at other funds. “A consumer team with a proven early stage track record, at a firm in leadership flux, is an obvious target,” said an industry insider.

But starting a new fund and running it successfully are two different things. Any new funds that come up from this churn will also need experienced hands to crack LP relationships.

“The churn right now is real, but to say that these newly floated funds founded by ex-partners and principals can shake the foundation of storied firms by poaching top dealmakers will be an exaggeration,” a founding partner of a consumer tech VC firm told us.

For Fireside Venture’s portfolio and its founders, the practical concern remains that of continuity. Many of them were backed by Kanwaljit Singh personally, or by the likes of Vinay Singh and Mohanty who have both left. The firm will have to reassign board seats and portfolio responsibilities as when all changes are final.

“The challenge here is to navigate the established relationships some of these partners will have with the portfolio founders and if these shoes are too big to fill for any newcomer,” an executive at a senior corporate advisory firm said.

Nothing about Fireside’s transition is final yet. Founder Kanwaljit Singh has not publicly announced any change in his role and his firm continues to invest actively out of its latest fund. Only those close to him are aware of his intentions. 

The signals, however, are hard to miss, especially after the departure of Vinay Singh and Mohanty, and word of churn inside the firm. Fireside Ventures now faces the test every founder-led fund eventually comes across. Can it convert its founder’s reputation into an institution? 

In the past, Kanwaljit Singh has said the firm should outlast any individual. The next few months will reveal whether that vision comes true.

Ask founders who have stayed away from quick commerce, and most of them will tell you the same thing: it is a cash furnace. Platforms take steep commissions, discounts never seem to end, and ad budgets disappear before a brand understands what sells. 

For a bootstrapped brand, these challenges alone ought to be reason enough to steer clear. But Durlabh Rawat, founder of Delhi NCR-based premium ghee brand Barosi, chose to dip his toe into the unforgiving waters of quick commerce in late 2022 with ghee, a product abundantly available in a market dominated by established FMCG giants.

Add to that the challenge of aggressive discounting. Also, with platforms taking at least a quarter of every sale from newer brands, breaking into quick commerce was no easy feat for a bootstrapped brand like Barosi. 

Four years on, Barosi, named after the traditional earthen hearth, is listed on 98% of Blinkit’s dark stores and sells across six rapid-delivery platforms. It closed FY26 with a revenue of ₹56 Cr and is now doing an annualised revenue run rate of ₹180 Cr. 

So, how did a bootstrapped dairy brand carve out a space for itself in quick commerce, despite stiff competition from deep-pocketed FMCG giants, limited dark-store shelf space and relentless pricing pressure?

In an exclusive conversation for The Quick Commerce Code by Inc42 and Nitro Commerce, Rawat explained how Barosi endured quick commerce’s challenges and shared the dos and don’ts for brands looking to crack the channel.

Barosi’s Dairy Tale

Rawat grew up in a modest household in a small village in Uttar Pradesh, where two cows and a goat tied in the backyard were as much a part of everyday life as the fields surrounding his home. Life moved slowly, with little to keep one occupied but plenty to dream about and even more to aspire to.

Like many young people from rural India, he had already dreamt of a glamorous big-city life. After completing his diploma in engineering, he joined Mahindra & Mahindra. But corporate life failed to hold his interest for long, and he soon found himself drawn back to his farming roots. 

Rawat decided to build a dairy business, a move that met with resistance at home. His parents, who had watched him work his way out of the village, saw it as a step backwards.

“They said you came out from cows and buffaloes, and you are going right back to where you wanted to leave from,” Rawat recalled.

Still, they had seen him build a career on his own and trusted that he would make it work. “They shared their point of view, but my conviction made them come round,” said Rawat. The brand started in 2016 as a subscription-led farm-to-home model, filling milk fresh from the farm each day and delivering it to customers the next morning.

Rawat zeroed in on Gurugram, a market with a growing appetite for artisanal dairy and proximity to Barosi’s farm in Pataudi.

After three years of operations, Rawat joined hands with Syed Shamoail Haque in 2019 to build the brand’s online presence. Haque later joined Barosi as a cofounder, taking over the brand’s digital growth.

During this time, the founders realised that they needed a hero product, something that would eventually become the mainstay of its portfolio. The two saw the potential in the brand’s range of premium A2 cow ghee, which was already selling like hotcakes on its website and Amazon. However, they wanted to bring their offering in front of a much larger audience.

Making The Quick Commerce Foray

Quick commerce seemed like the obvious next step for Barosi. But there was a catch. The platforms weren’t convinced consumers would turn to a 10-minute delivery app to buy premium cow ghee. 

“We were constantly reaching out to them, but our pitch fell on deaf ears,” Rawat said.

We made some headway in February 2023, when we appeared on Shark Tank India. Soon after the episode aired, Blinkit founder Albinder Dhindsa announced on LinkedIn that the platform would onboard brands featured on the show.

This time, the founders struck while the iron was hot, seizing the opportunity to get Barosi onto quick commerce.

In April 2023, it secured a listing on Blinkit’s 30-minute delivery service for long-tail products. But securing a spot on Blinkit’s main board, where products were stocked in regular dark stores for 10-minute deliveries, remained the next big hurdle.

Unlike many brands that rework their products to figure out what would work on quick commerce, the founders did not sweat much and listed the same SKUs that were selling online. 

“The priority was not to reinvent the product but to widen our reach. We needed better distribution reach, which we got from quick commerce,” Rawat said.

The numbers soon began to follow. Barosi graduated to Blinkit’s main board, followed by Instamart in June 2023 and Zepto in December. Barely eight months after its Blinkit debut, the brand had crossed ₹1 Cr in monthly quick commerce sales.

Rawat attributes much of this to the team’s close reading of the channel. Barosi tracked ad performance across different times of the day and found that demand peaked in the mornings, evenings and on weekends. 

It first built a stronghold in NCR, took the same playbook to Bengaluru and then expanded city by city. “When our numbers started coming in, quick commerce platforms also realised that a new brand was getting so much traction,” Rawat said. 

Looking back, Rawat points to three factors that worked in Barosi’s favour: a differentiated product, a brand story compelling enough to get customers looking for it, and timing. 

Balancing The Quick Commerce Economics

The writing is on the wall: winning customers in quick commerce comes at a price, especially for new brands. Platforms extract a much larger retail margin from newer brands than they do from established FMCG players. 

“If you average out retail margins, Blinkit takes at least 25%, but legacy brands like Amul or ITC won’t give them more than single digits,” he said. 

Established FMCG companies have the scale and bargaining power to negotiate lower margins. On the other hand, quick commerce platforms have an incentive to push newer brands, from which they can extract better margins. But paying for distribution is one thing and allowing platforms to undermine a brand’s pricing is quite another.

Rawat said some platforms started discounting Barosi’s products so aggressively that the company eventually had to stop supplying them. 

The founder considers a 10-15% discount reasonable, with the cost shared between the brand and the platform. Barosi contributes around 7-8%, while the platform chips in with another 5-6%. He sees this as a shared investment in bringing customers on board, rather than a price cut that one side must bear entirely.

He said that for a bootstrapped company, keeping prices fair is as much a customer acquisition strategy as it is a margin decision. 

An analysis by Zodiac AI by Nitro Commerce found that Barosi maintained an average on-shelf availability of 94.7% on Blinkit between 31 October and 3 November 2026, with a weighted average MRP of ₹2,243.

Building Dark-Store Distribution Muscle

A dark store has limited shelf space, and every product has to earn its place. Platforms, therefore, favour brands and SKUs that move quickly and consistently.

Barosi entered quick commerce with two principal SKUs: 500 ml and 1-litre packs of A2 cow ghee. The 1-litre pack remains its bestseller, but the portfolio has since expanded to around 25 SKUs, including oils. One unexpected hit has been the 5-litre ghee tub. Initially introduced on Barosi’s website, it attracted enough demand that Blinkit asked the brand to list it on the platform.

It also marks a shift from what customers typically buy on quick commerce. A 5-litre tub is hardly an impulse purchase. Its traction suggests customers are increasingly using these platforms for planned, higher-value grocery purchases also.

Barosi’s average order value across channels is around ₹1,100, with quick commerce adding another ₹200-300, according to Rawat. The company also sees repeat purchases from 20-22% of its quick commerce customers in the first month, rising to around 28% by the second.

Demand is not confined to metros. Tier II cities now account for roughly 40% of Barosi’s business. Lucknow and Jaipur regularly feature among its seven largest markets, while Chandigarh, Panchkula and Mohali together rank among the top five.

More Platforms Over More Products

By 2025, Barosi faced a key question: where would its next phase of growth come from? Having already invested in a new ghee manufacturing plant, the company decided to focus on expanding its ghee business rather than entering new product categories.

“If we invested capex on the ghee side, we have to build ghee first on different channels. So the product question was solved there,” Rawat said. The next step, therefore, was to expand its presence across more sales channels.

Amazon and Flipkart were small channels for Barosi until recently. A year ago, Rawat said, the two together contributed only 7-8% of revenue. That changed after both launched their own rapid-delivery services. Amazon Now and Flipkart Minutes have become Barosi’s second- and third-largest channels this year, overtaking both Instamart and Zepto. 

Together, Amazon and Flipkart now account for about 23% of Barosi’s revenue.

Rawat shared that Amazon Now accounts for about 70% of Barosi’s Amazon sales, and Flipkart Minutes for about 74-75% of its Flipkart sales. Rawat saw an opportunity in this shift. If the large marketplaces were moving into rapid delivery, Barosi could spread its risk without leaving the channel that had built it. 

“We realised it’s more important that if we divide our dependence on three quick commerce channels, our risk will be optimised,” he said.

Rawat said Blinkit, Instamart and Zepto, which once accounted for 90-100% of Barosi’s business, now account for about half. 

Alongside Amazon Now and Flipkart Minutes, the other growth drivers this year are BigBasket and FirstClub. “BigBasket does very good numbers for us,” he said.

Barosi is now considering markets beyond rapid delivery. Rawat said the company deliberately stayed away from offline retail, as it would demand too much energy and capital. 

“In a space where consumer momentum is clear, where consumers continuously want things in 10 minutes through different channels, going against that wouldn’t be wise,” he said.

Rawat, however, said that exports will be the company’s priority this year.

Barosi’s ₹1,000 Cr Run

On its current trajectory, Barosi looks like one of quick commerce’s more compelling consumer-brand success stories. Its revenue grew from about ₹11 Cr in FY24 to ₹32-34 Cr in FY25 and ₹56 Cr in FY26. Rawat told Inc42 that the company is now running at an annualised revenue run rate of ₹180 Cr, which translates into monthly sales of roughly ₹15 Cr. 

“We have set our sights on growing more than 5X in yearly ARR to ₹1,000 Cr in the next five years,” the founder said.

Barosi’s growth so far has come without the cushion of external capital, a distinction few consumer brands in its category can claim. Along the way, it has built a new ghee manufacturing plant, grown its workforce to around 70 employees, supplemented by nearly 100 off-roll workers at the plant, and established distribution across seven quick commerce platforms. 

It has also managed to stay profitable. The company reported a profit after tax margin of 2-3% in FY26, which Rawat expects to improve to 8-9% this financial year.

But a business built on quick commerce comes with its own set of dependencies. For example, Barosi relies on platforms that, by Rawat’s account, take at least a quarter of the selling price from brands like his. That leaves limited room to absorb platform commissions, fund discounts, protect gross margins and still expand profitability.

Then, there is the question of control. Barosi’s visibility and reach depend heavily on platform algorithms and category teams, which decide how much dark-store shelf space a brand gets and where its products appear. 

And, perhaps most importantly, there is the concentration risk. With nearly 90% of its revenue coming from a single product category, Barosi has little room for complacency. New competitors, pricing pressure or a platform choosing to push its own ghee label could all put that dominance to the test.

Rawat is looking to address some of these vulnerabilities by spreading the company’s exposure across more platforms and building its export business. The brand is also preparing to launch a new product range.

But what got Barosi this far may not be enough to take it to the next level. As competition intensifies and quick commerce platforms tighten their grip on distribution, the brand will need new ways to grow without compromising its margins. Therefore, its next quest for ₹1,000 Cr in ARR will be worth witnessing.

Now, the question is whether ShareChat’s new revenue mix represents a clearing towards a path to profitability or is it a temporary shift before the next product comes along, as we have seen throughout the company’s lifetime. 

Subscriptions Turn Fruitful 

When QuickTV launched in February 2025, ShareChat was entering a new territory, chasing subscription revenue. Thus far in its lifetime, it relied on incidental revenue in its apps, whether it was in-app transactions by users or ad revenue from brands. QuickTV was the first push for direct revenue.

Besides QuickTV, the company also offers paid subscriptions through Moj Premium, but the latter is not yet a compelling revenue line. Microdrama seems to have hit the ground running. 

However, building a subscription business comes with a different set of challenges, particularly when it comes to acquiring paying customers.

Charan claimed that while Moj spends around ₹14–15 to get a user to download the app, QuickTV subscription platforms can spend 6-7x higher to acquire a paying customer as the cost can be recouped through the subscription model. 

This makes it easier to acquire users profitably over the short term but more expensive over a long period. That long-term cost can take a toll on QuickTV and other microdrama platforms. Subscription-centric apps like microdrama platforms have to continuously invest in content to retain customers, and this can get especially expensive when there are multiple apps competing and offering similar microdramas and storylines. 

While AI is actively changing how microdramas are being made and therefore reducing costs, the economics are still not in the favour of the platform. It is estimated that producing a minute of microdrama using human actors costs ₹20,000–₹25,000, compared with ₹7,000–₹8,000 using AI tools and generative video models. 

While lower production costs could help platforms scale their content libraries and reduce long-term retention costs, they could also make it easier for competitors to produce similar shows, putting greater pressure on differentiation and even that initial customer acquisition.

The latter also gives Mohalla Tech an opportunity to identify popular themes and formats before committing larger production budgets.

Charan said QuickTV has operated without burning cash since launch, though this remains to be verified since we were not given exact costs for QuickTV in terms of marketing and promotional spends. 

But Mohalla Tech is at a crossroads right now because of how QuickTV has performed. 

Subscriptions have revived its topline growth, but sustaining that momentum will require investing in retargeting, a clear content strategy and pipeline as well as other incremental spending to improve the subscriber experience, which will play a role in retention. 

If the past year has shown anything it is that users will pay for microdramas, but to keep them paying every month requires a lot more pieces moving in sync than just the right marketing pitch.

When it comes to QuickTV, AI is becoming central to monetisation and bringing in cost-efficiency on the production side. Not only is it playing a role in improving content discovery and therefore driving conversions to subscriptions, but it can help improve ad targeting.

Though this number cannot be verified as YouTube’s average ad revenue per user is not disclosed.  

ShareChat is also experimenting with how brands advertise within microdramas using integrated storytelling or branded content. Other options include ads between episodes, programmatic inventory and interactive shopping formats backed by marketplaces. 

What Will Carry ShareChat Forward?

Like we mentioned above, the caveat to ShareChat’s return to growth is that it has yet to result in lower losses. Charan attributed the forex losses and roughly ₹548 Cr in interest provisions to dollar-denominated financing instruments, describing the latter as non-cash expenses. However, sustaining this momentum will become increasingly challenging as competition intensifies. Homegrown microdrama platforms such as EloElo, Kuku TV and Story TV are competing for paying subscribers, while global players including ReelShort and DramaBox are targeting the Indian market. 

Larger entertainment platforms, including JioHotstar, ZEE5 and Amazon MX Player, are also expanding into short-form storytelling. This could push up customer acquisition costs and content spending, particularly for QuickTV’s subscription-led business. 

Consolidation can very well be on the cards as competition intensifies and investors increasingly scrutinise contribution margins. 

As we see with the OTT streaming industry, India is home to around 100 Mn – 150 Mn people who regularly pay for content online and even there a kind of subscription fatigue has set in, with users preferring to stick to one platform or only buying subscriptions through telecom bundles. 

It also adds more pressure on ShareChat, Moj and QuickTV to explore ways in which users can seamlessly switch between streaming, creator-generated content and chatrooms. 

Interestingly, the three Mohalla Tech platforms do not currently act as interconnected funnels. This will be a critical piece of operating leverage for the company in the long run.

Connecting this to the silos of ShareChat and Moj will give the company more bargaining power with advertisers as well as creators.  

This is why while ShareChat, Moj and QuickTV’s growth is undeniable, a stronger push by platforms such as YouTube or Meta could challenge that position because of their incumbency and the inherent moats that come with that position. 

But the milestones of FY26, the revenue trajectory and the fact that the company now reaches millions of social media users more frequently through three different apps offer plenty of reasons for optimism, even amid these questions.
